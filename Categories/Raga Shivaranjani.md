**Raga Shivaranjani (ശിവരഞ്ജിനി)** is a very expressive raga in Indian classical music, especially known for creating a feeling of **pathos, longing, tenderness, and melancholy**.

### What does the name mean?

**Shiva + Ranjani**

- **Shiva** = Lord Shiva
- **Ranjani** = that which pleases, delights, or enchants

So **Shivaranjani** can roughly be understood as:

> **“That which pleases Shiva”** or **“the one that enchants Shiva.”**


### The notes

In the commonly used **Carnatic** form, **Shivaranjani** is:

**Arohanam:**  
`S R₂ G₂ P D₂ S`

**Avarohanam:**  
`S D₂ P G₂ R₂ S`

So, importantly, it uses:

**Sa, Ri₂, Ga₂, Pa, Dha₂, Sa**

and **does not use Ma or Ni**.

If we take **C as Sa**, that becomes approximately:

**C – D – E♭ – G – A – C**

and descending:

**C – A – G – E♭ – D – C**

That minor-third (**Ga₂ / E♭**) is a major contributor to its characteristic **plaintive** sound.

### Why does it sound sad?

The interesting thing is that Shivaranjani isn't simply "a sad scale."

The emotional effect comes from **how the notes are used**, particularly:

**Sa → Ri → Ga₂**

The movement toward the minor third gives a distinctly plaintive colour:

`C → D → E♭`

while the absence of **Ma and Ni** makes the melodic space feel relatively sparse.

The movement

`Ga₂ → Ri₂ → Sa`

can also create a strong feeling of **returning or yearning toward the tonic**.

That's why compositions in Shivaranjani can sound:

- yearning
- nostalgic
- lonely
- devotional
- romantic
- emotionally vulnerable

depending on tempo, instrumentation and phrasing.

### One important distinction

You'll sometimes see **Shivaranjani classified differently in Carnatic and Hindustani contexts**. The Hindustani Shivranjani commonly has a related but somewhat different treatment, so don't assume every song labelled "Shivaranjani" follows exactly the same grammar.

And this connects directly to what we were discussing about **legato melodies**: Shivaranjani is particularly effective when the singer **connects the notes smoothly and ornaments the transitions**. Played mechanically on a keyboard, the scale can sound fairly ordinary. Sung with proper phrasing, suddenly the emotional character appears.

A good example to study is **“Kanne Kalaimane”** in Tamil film music, which uses a Shivaranjani-based melodic language.
